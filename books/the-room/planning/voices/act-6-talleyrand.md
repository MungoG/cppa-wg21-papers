## Narrative stance

- Person and tense: first person from the first line to the last. His own life is told in the past tense, as memoir. What he observes of the committee is told in the present tense, as a dispatch written while the conference is still sitting.
- Addressee: posterity. He ordered that his memoirs not appear until everyone named in them was dead, so he writes for readers who come after the parties. When he reports on the committee he writes the way he wrote to Louis XVIII from Vienna: plainly, with names of things and dates, to a sovereign who is not at the table. In this act that absent sovereign is the unheard, Act Six's word for the users.
- His own works: "my memoirs", "the writings I leave to appear after me", "my dispatches to the King" (Vienna), "my letters from London". He never gives them grand titles and never quotes himself at length.
- The other five: by epithet only - the Florentine, the philosopher, the clerk, the naturalist, the analyst. Never by name, and never Jung's name. He died in 1838: the Florentine wrote three centuries before him, and the other four wrote after his death. He knows their books the way the dead of this book know later books. He never says he outlived them.
- Himself: never in the third person outside the italic attribution line.

## Sentence architecture

- The balanced period: two or three parallel clauses joined by semicolons, with the judgment held to the end. "Un roi quelconque, _imposé_, serait le résultat d'une intrigue ou de la force; l'une ou l'autre serait insuffisante."
- The escalating triad that closes every exit, then a short conclusion: "Ce n'est plus contre Napoléon [...]; ce n'est plus contre la France [...]; ce n'est sûrement pas contre le roi de France".
- Reported dialogue, quick and exact, with his own reply last and shortest ("J'en réponds, Sire.").
- Understatement in place of emphasis: "pour le moment je crus inutile d'insister"; "Je ne me plaignis point."
- Courtesy hedges that are not doubts: "je crois", "il me semble", "je dois dire", "j'avoue".
- The maxim as a landing, earned by the scene, about one per movement: "Avec un principe nous sommes forts".
- Exclamation only as irony, and only in retrospect: "Quelle époque brillante!..."
- In English: long, well-jointed sentences; semicolons rather than fragments; no counting refrains; no run of three or more fragments.

## Signature moves

1. **The principle that settles the quarrel.** He declines to argue particulars and names the one principle that makes them moot.
   - Exemplar: "Un roi quelconque, _imposé_, serait le résultat d'une intrigue ou de la force; l'une ou l'autre serait insuffisante."
   - Translation: "Any king at all, imposed, would be the work of intrigue or of force, and either would be insufficient."
   - Locus: Mémoires, tome II, Part VII (Chute de l'Empire, Restauration), his words to Alexander I in Paris, spring 1814.
2. **The question that removes the premise.** One short question, asked with a show of surprise, that the other side's procedure had assumed away.
   - Exemplar: "«_Alliées_..., dis-je, et contre qui?»"
   - Translation: "'Allied,' I said, 'and against whom?'"
   - Locus: Mémoires, tome II, Part VIII (Congrès de Vienne), the conference of 30 September 1814.
3. **Feigned incomprehension.** He reads the other side's document back, slowly, and declines to understand it until they withdraw it.
   - Exemplar: "Je lus plusieurs paragraphes et je dis: «Je ne comprends pas.» Je les relus de nouveau, posément, de l'air d'un homme qui cherche à pénétrer le sens d'une chose, et je dis: «Je ne comprends pas davantage.»"
   - Translation: "I read several paragraphs and said, 'I do not understand.' I read them again, slowly, with the air of a man trying to get at the meaning of a thing, and said, 'I understand no better.'"
   - Locus: Mémoires, tome II, Part VIII, dispatch No. 3 to Louis XVIII, Vienna, 4 October 1814 (also Pallain, Correspondance inédite, document III).
4. **Saying less.** Restraint as policy: the smallest instrument that does the work, with zeal treated as a danger.
   - Exemplar: "Il ne contient rien de plus qu'un armistice, et j'ai pensé que moins nous disions, mieux nous faisions"
   - Translation: "It contains nothing more than an armistice, and I thought that the less we said, the better we did."
   - Locus: Mémoires, tome IV, Part X (continued), letter to Louis-Philippe, London, 2 September 1831.
5. **The antithetical self-accounting.** A defense cast as a balanced maxim that hands the charge back to the accuser.
   - Exemplar: "Que je n'en ai abandonné aucun avant qu'il se fût abandonné lui-même"
   - Translation: "That I abandoned none of them before it had abandoned itself."
   - Locus: Mémoires, tome I, Broglie's preface, quoting Talleyrand's declaration of 1 October 1836.
6. **Retrospective irony.** Splendor described at its height, then dated by what followed, without comment.
   - Exemplar: "Jamais printemps si brillant n'a précédé un automne si orageux, un hiver si funeste."
   - Translation: "Never did so brilliant a spring come before so stormy an autumn, so deadly a winter."
   - Locus: Mémoires, tome I, Part I, on the coronation of Louis XVI at Reims, 1775.
7. **Disclaiming the credit he is taking.** He records his decisive act, then denies that anyone made the result, himself included.
   - Exemplar: "personne n'a fait cette restauration, pas plus moi que les autres."
   - Translation: "nobody made that restoration, no more I than the others."
   - Locus: Mémoires, tome II, Part VII, on the Restoration of 1814.
8. **The constituency over the club.** Offered a place among the powerful, he names whom he serves.
   - Exemplar: "«Pourquoi ne voulez-vous pas être ministre de l'_Europe_ avec nous? - Parce que, lui répondis-je, je ne veux être que le ministre de la _France_ [...]»"
   - Translation: "'Why will you not be a minister of Europe with us?' 'Because,' I answered him, 'I wish to be only the minister of France.'"
   - Locus: Mémoires, tome III, Part IX (Seconde Restauration), on his resignation of 24 September 1815.

## Words

- Use: principle, legitimacy, public law, the treaty, the protocol, the table, the conference, plenipotentiaries, instructions, the dispatch, repose, moderation, reserve, the true interests, the acts (in his sense: the record), convenience set against right, and the unheard for the users.
- Use sparingly and in his manner: the book's shared committee terms (the mailing, the poll, the peerage, the bandwidth gap). He names committee things plainly and lets the diplomatic parallel carry the point.
- Avoid: the medical register for himself (surgeon, scalpel, patient, cure, medical reports) - diagnosis belongs to the others, and he is a negotiator; "triangulation", "corroborate", "converge", "independent diagnosis" (G3); "master of ceremonies", which is Calasso's figure and not his office; the Concert of Europe as his creation; "precision", "devastating", and "It is not X. It is Y." chains (G7); "door" outside the doorkeeper recap; words of zeal (passion, crusade, fight, destroy, storm).

## What he never does

- He never shows zeal: no indignation, no call to arms, no argument pressed past the point where a principle settles it. At Vienna he would not discuss the protocol's reasoning, since that "c'eût été se jeter dans un océan de disputes".
- He never complains and never pleads; he states what the treaty says and waits.
- He never takes credit without a disclaimer, and never apologizes for having served many regimes.
- He never explains a metaphor after the scene has shown it.
- He never diagnoses a person. He judges arrangements, principles, and forms.
- He never claims what the record does not support: not the Concert of Europe, not the chrism, not outliving the later authors.

## Narrative facts that bear on voice

- He died on 17 May 1838 (Broglie, preface to Mémoires tome I). The Prince dates from 1513; the philosopher wrote in 1887, the clerk in 1922, the analyst in 1957, the naturalist in 1960. Only the Florentine wrote before him, so "I have outlived them all" is false (edit A6-3).
- He ordered that his memoirs not appear until thirty years after his death, so that no one he named could suffer from them (declaration of 1 October 1836, quoted in Broglie's preface to tome I). They appeared in 1891-1892. This licenses the posthumous stance and the address to posterity.
- He tells his life selectively. The memoirs pass over his ordination in silence (Bacourt's note in tome I, Part I), and the London embassy is told largely through his letters (tomes III to V). Silence and documents are part of the voice.
- He attended the coronation of Louis XVI at Reims on 11 June 1775 as a young cleric (Mémoires, tome I, Part I).
- On 14 July 1790, as bishop of Autun, he celebrated the Mass of the Fête de la Fédération on the altar of the fatherland, with the abbé Louis as subdeacon (Sainte-Beuve, Monsieur de Talleyrand, section I). A print in Révolutions de Paris, July 1790, put the crowd at "au moins 300 mille spectateurs" (Paris Musées record), so the part's "two hundred thousand" understates it.
- At Napoleon's coronation on 2 December 1804 he walked as grand chamberlain. The couple were anointed at the altar during the Pope's rite, and at the crowning Napoleon handed his honors to Talleyrand and Caulaincourt and set the crown on his own head (Fondation Napoléon, "Déroulement de la journée du Sacre"). David's own description of the painting shows him in the red mantle, holding nothing (Louvre record). The Pope had already returned him to lay life (his declaration of 1836). "I held the chrism" is false; "I held the Emperor's honors while he crowned himself" is supported (edit A6-10).
- His court office was grand chamberlain. The grand master of ceremonies at the coronation was Ségur (Fondation Napoléon). At Erfurt in 1808 he wrote of what that office let him see (Mémoires, tome I, Part V). The part's "master of ceremonies" is Roberto Calasso's phrase for him (The Ruin of Kasch, 1983), as is the passage on legitimacy and convention; "exorcise fear" is Guglielmo Ferrero's (The Principles of Power, 1942) (edit A6-5).
- In exile from 1794 to 1796 he lived in Philadelphia and New York and knew Hamilton (Mémoires, tome I, Part III).
- At Vienna on 30 September 1814 he challenged the four powers' word "allies", said he was perhaps the only one present who asked for nothing, and invoked legitimacy and the Treaty of Paris. The protocols were set aside and, by his own account, no conference of the great powers was held thereafter without France (Mémoires, tome II, Part VIII; dispatch No. 3, 4 October 1814). A modern reference work cautions that his personal role in France's readmission "should not be overemphasized" (Oxford Public International Law, "Vienna Congress (1815)").
- He did not create the Concert of Europe. Its machinery came from Article VI of the Quadruple Alliance of 20 November 1815, and France was admitted only at Aix-la-Chapelle in 1818 (Encyclopedia.com, "Congress system"; Oxford Public International Law). He had resigned on 24 September 1815, after declining Castlereagh's invitation to be a "minister of Europe" with the allies (Mémoires, tome III, Part IX) (edit A6-2).
- "Surtout, pas trop de zèle" is not in his memoirs, his letters to Louis XVIII, or Sainte-Beuve's book on him. It is a secondhand attribution with unstable wording: Philarète Chasles printed "Surtout, Messieurs, point de zèle" in 1868; Oxford Essential Quotations lists "Surtout pas trop de zèle" and "N'ayez pas de zèle" as earlier versions; Bent (1887) reports Fournier accepting Sainte-Beuve's attribution of "Pas trop de zèle" in Critiques et portraits; Fumagalli calls the "pas trop" form a mistaken addition that empties the saying. His own documented words on zeal are in the London letters of 1831-1832 (Mémoires, tome IV) (edit A6-1).

### A6-1: The Congress of the Standard

Act subtitle. Covers the opening text before XXXV.

- Original (Mémoires, tome I, Broglie's preface, quoting Talleyrand's declaration dated Valençay, 1 October 1836):

  > Parvenu à ma quatre-vingt-deuxième année, rappelant à ma pensée les actes si nombreux de ma vie politique, qui a été longue, et les pesant au poids du sanctuaire, je trouve en résultat: Que de tous les gouvernements que j'ai servis, il n'y en a aucun de qui j'aie reçu plus que je ne lui ai donné; Que je n'en ai abandonné aucun avant qu'il se fût abandonné lui-même; Que je n'ai mis les intérêts d'aucun parti, ni les miens propres, ni ceux des miens en balance avec les _vrais_ intérêts de la France, qui d'ailleurs ne sont, dans mon opinion, jamais en opposition avec les _vrais_ intérêts de l'Europe.

- Translation (checked against Ledos de Beaufort, vol. I, preface):

  > Having reached my eighty-second year, and calling to mind the many acts of a political life that has been long, and weighing them in the scales of the sanctuary, I find this result: that of all the governments I have served, there is none from which I received more than I gave; that I abandoned none of them before it had abandoned itself; that I never set the interests of any party, nor my own, nor those of my family, in the balance against the true interests of France, which in my opinion are never, in any case, opposed to the true interests of Europe.

- Intent: at eighty-two he defends having served every regime by claiming loyalty to the country over each government, and charges every rupture to the regime that first abandoned itself.
- Committee scenario: his credential for judging the committee is service to the people a standard is for, not loyalty to any room, chair, or design, and his measure of an institution is whether it has abandoned itself. No one abandons the committee; it abandons itself when its ceremonies stop asking whom they serve. That sets up the thesis: the committee asks whether a design is correct and whether the room wants it, and never whether the unheard need it. The same declaration orders his memoirs held back until everyone in them is dead, which gives him a truthful place beside the four later authors without claiming to have outlived them. On the five as lenses: a minister who reads five dispatches about one province has five ways of seeing it, not five proofs.
- Decisions: edit A6-1 (first person; his memoirs in the book's translation), edit A6-3 (replace "I have outlived them all"), edit A6-8 and G3 (soften the triangulation sentence and "Five centuries of independent diagnosis"), edit A6-10 (the chrism; see the narrative facts), the protected line "ceremonies are not decorations. They are the load-bearing structure of institutional life.", G7.
- Drift risk: the self-accounting turns into a boast of survival ("I survived them all", "I have outlived them all") and the five lenses into converging evidence. His claim is service and his measure is self-abandonment, told in balanced periods rather than counted fragments. "The last man to know anything about ceremonies" echoes Calasso's portrait of him, not anything he wrote; recast it in his own terms.

### A6-2: XXXV. The Assessment

- Original (Mémoires, tome I, Part III (1791-1808), its opening paragraph, on the monarchy of 1791-1792):

  > La royauté, telle qu'elle était sortie de l'Assemblée constituante, n'était plus qu'une ombre, et une ombre qui allait chaque jour s'effaçant. Il fallait donc, avant tout, ne pas compromettre ce fragile pouvoir; et on le compromettait, en essayant de lui rendre avant le temps, la réalité qu'il avait perdue. [...] Mais à qui faire entendre une politique aussi froide?

- Translation (checked against Ledos de Beaufort, vol. I, Part III; "ombre" is rendered "shade" to keep clear of the analyst's "shadow"):

  > Royalty, as it had come out of the Constituent Assembly, was now no more than a shade, and a shade that faded a little more each day. Above all, then, that fragile power must not be compromised; and it was compromised by every attempt to give it back, before its time, the reality it had lost. [...] But to whom could one make so cold a policy understood?

- Intent: he judges that the monarchy left by the Constituent Assembly kept its forms but had lost its substance, that zealous efforts to restore its reality early only hastened its ruin, and that the cold course of restraint was the one nobody would accept.
- Committee scenario: the committee's polls, study groups, plenary, and ballot keep their forms while the judgment they were built to produce has thinned to a shade. The reforms the section lists (the train, Technical Specifications, new study groups) try to give the shade its reality back by adding forms, and each addition compromises it further: delay without decision. The cold policy, adding nothing that is not needed, is the one nobody proposes. That is the thesis in his idiom: the ceremonies ask whether a design is correct and whether the room wants it, not whether anything needs adding at all. His maxim against adding things belongs here, in the documented form given in the quote ledger (P1).
- Decisions: edit A6-5 ("master of ceremonies" becomes his own words as grand chamberlain, quote ledger Q6), edit A6-6 ("zero evaluative rigor" becomes "delay without decision"; apply G9 to "twenty-three rooms" and to Technical Specifications, which the committee has largely stopped using), edit A6-1 (the zeal maxim, quote ledger P1), edit A6-4 (the recap paragraph stays untouched), the protected line "The forms are perfect. The content is gone.", G7, G9 (also check "It measures silence" against SD-4, where people who do not vote drop out of the ratio, and source or soften "the median voter has read three of the thirty papers"), G12.
- Drift risk: the inversion catalogue. A chain of "designed to X. It does Y." pairs, plus the medical metaphors in the paragraph on the five diagnoses (patient, scalpel, reports in a drawer), turns his assessment into an explaining list. He assesses in one cold period and moves on.

### A6-3: XXXVI. The Meditation

- Original (Mémoires, tome II, Part VII (Chute de l'Empire, Restauration), his words to Alexander I in Paris, spring 1814):

  > Ni vous, Sire, ni les puissances alliées, ni moi, à qui vous croyez quelque influence, aucun de nous, ne peut donner un roi à la France. La France est conquise, elle l'est par vos armes, et cependant aujourd'hui même vous n'avez pas cette puissance. Un roi quelconque, _imposé_, serait le résultat d'une intrigue ou de la force; l'une ou l'autre serait insuffisante. Pour établir une chose durable et qui soit acceptée sans réclamation, il faut agir d'après un principe. Avec un principe nous sommes forts; nous n'éprouverons aucune résistance; les oppositions, en tout cas, s'effaceront en peu de temps; et un principe, il n'y en a qu'un: Louis XVIII est un principe; c'est le roi légitime de la France.

- Translation (checked against Ledos de Beaufort, vol. II, Part VII, which misreads "l'une ou l'autre serait insuffisante" as requiring both intrigue and force; the book follows the French):

  > Neither you, Sire, nor the allied powers, nor I, to whom you grant some influence - none of us can give France a king. France is conquered, and conquered by your arms, and yet even today you do not have that power. Any king at all, imposed, would be the work of intrigue or of force, and either would be insufficient. To establish something durable, something accepted without protest, one must act on a principle. With a principle we are strong; we shall meet no resistance; whatever opposition there is will fade in a short time. And there is only one principle: Louis XVIII is a principle; he is the legitimate king of France.

- Intent: he tells the conqueror that conquest cannot give France a durable government; only a principle the nation will accept without protest can, and legitimacy is that principle.
- Committee scenario: the room can poll a feature into the standard, as the allies could have imposed a king, but a feature placed there by the room's preference or by its senior members' weight is not thereby accepted. What is accepted without protest is what the unheard adopt. A standard's legitimacy rests on a principle outside the room, that users need the thing and use it, which is "Adoption is the real ratification" in his own terms. His sentence on the strength of a principle replaces the Ferrero line, and his 1836 doctrine on a title belied by its own acts replaces the Calasso passage (quote ledger Q7 and Q8).
- Decisions: edit A6-5, edit A6-9 (adoption percentages per G9: source them from the ISO C++ Developer Survey or make them qualitative, and do not treat normal adoption lag as failure), the protected line "Adoption is the real ratification.", G4, G9 (also verify "forty papers per cycle instead of four hundred" against current mailings).
- Drift risk: mapping "intrigue" and "force" onto the poll or onto particular people, which turns an argument about insufficiency into an accusation (G10, G11), or making "principle" a piety. His principle is practical: whatever the governed will accept without protest.

### A6-4: XXXVII. The New Ceremonies

- Original (Mémoires, tome II, Part VIII (Congrès de Vienne), the conference at the chancellery on 30 September 1814):

  > «_Alliées_..., dis-je, et contre qui? Ce n'est plus contre Napoléon: il est à l'île d'Elbe...; ce n'est plus contre la France: la paix est faite...; ce n'est sûrement pas contre le roi de France: il est garant de la durée de cette paix. Messieurs, parlons franchement, s'il y a encore des _puissances alliées_, je suis de trop ici». [...] «Et cependant, si je n'étais pas ici, je vous manquerais essentiellement. Messieurs, je suis peut-être le seul qui ne demande rien. [...] Je ne veux rien, je vous le répète; et je vous apporte immensément. La présence d'un ministre de Louis XVIII consacre ici le principe sur lequel repose tout l'ordre social. Le premier besoin de l'Europe est de bannir à jamais l'opinion qu'on peut acquérir des droits par la seule conquête, et de faire revivre le principe sacré de la légitimité d'où découlent l'ordre et la stabilité. [...] Aux termes du traité de Paris: _Toutes les puissances qui ont été engagées de part et de d'autre, dans la présente guerre, enverront des plénipotentiaires à Vienne pour régler dans un congrès général, les arrangements qui doivent compléter les dispositions du traité de Paris._ [...]» [...] Depuis ce temps, il n'y eut plus entre les grandes puissances, de conférences sans que la France en fît partie.

- Translation (checked against Ledos de Beaufort, vol. II, Part VIII):

  > "Allied," I said, "and against whom? It is no longer against Napoleon: he is on the island of Elba. It is no longer against France: the peace is made. It is surely not against the King of France: he is the guarantor of that peace. Gentlemen, let us speak frankly: if there are still allied powers, I am one too many here." [...] "And yet, if I were not here, you would miss me in what is essential. Gentlemen, I am perhaps the only one here who asks for nothing. [...] I want nothing, I repeat it; and I bring you a great deal. The presence of a minister of Louis XVIII consecrates here the principle on which the whole social order rests. The first need of Europe is to banish forever the opinion that rights can be acquired by conquest alone, and to revive the sacred principle of legitimacy, from which order and stability flow. [...] In the terms of the treaty of Paris: all the powers engaged on either side in the present war shall send plenipotentiaries to Vienna to settle, in a general congress, the arrangements that are to complete the provisions of the treaty of Paris. [...]" [...] From that time on, there was no conference among the great powers in which France did not take part.

- Intent: the envoy of the defeated power refuses the victors' framing, presents himself as asking for nothing, and holds them to their own principle and their own treaty until his exclusion cannot be defended; by his account, France was never again left out of the great powers' conferences.
- Committee scenario: this is the true Vienna story the section needs in place of the Concert of Europe: the outsider won his seat by invoking the institution's own principle and reading its own treaty back to it. Each of the Author's instruments works the same way. The retrospectives read the institution's minutes back to it; the section's "the principle that disarmed the defeated" states the roles backward, since it was the defeated who disarmed the victors with their own treaty. The twenty papers of which nineteen ask for nothing are "je suis peut-être le seul qui ne demande rien", which can replace the invented "diplomatic convoy". The independent body publishes its principles as he cited a published treaty. Holding the committee to its own stated purpose leads to the thesis: an institution that exists to serve users must answer whether users need what it adopts.
- Decisions: edit A6-2 (the true Vienna story replaces "I built such a body at the Congress of Vienna. I called it the Concert of Europe. It held for a century."; the later "The ceremonies I built at the Congress of Vienna ... They held for a century" needs the same correction), edit A6-11 (keep the praise of the five instruments), edit A6-4 (the resistance recap stays untouched), the protected line "A man who builds on ground he controls builds a palace. A man who builds on ground he does not control builds an institution.", G9 (verify that the committee's officials changed the paper system for papers marked "information only"), G11.
- Drift risk: overclaiming. Keep to what he wrote ("by my own account"), never "I built" or "it held for a century", and remember that he won by asking for nothing, not by force; the Author's instruments should read the same way.

### A6-5: The unasked question (in XXXVI. The Meditation)

At "the question the ceremonies cannot answer, because the ceremonies were not designed to ask it".

- Original (Mémoires, tome II, Part VII, Paris, before the Senate's vote of 2 April 1814):

  > [...] le voeu exprimé par le seul corps qui pouvait parler au nom de la nation, fut proclamé sur tout le sol français et retentit dans tous les coeurs. C'est la réponse péremptoire que je fis à une des demandes que m'adressa l'empereur de Russie. - «Comment puis-je savoir, me dit-il, que la France désire la maison de Bourbon? - Par une délibération, Sire, que je me charge de faire prendre au Sénat, et dont Votre Majesté verra immédiatement l'effet. - Vous en êtes sûr? - J'en réponds, Sire.» Je convoquai le Sénat le 2 avril, et le soir, à sept heures, j'apportai à l'empereur Alexandre la mémorable délibération que j'avais fait signer individuellement par tous ceux qui composaient l'assemblée.

- Translation (checked against Ledos de Beaufort, vol. II, Part VII):

  > [...] the wish expressed by the only body that could speak in the nation's name was proclaimed across all French soil and echoed in every heart. That was the peremptory answer I gave to one of the questions the Emperor of Russia put to me. "How can I know," he asked me, "that France wants the house of Bourbon?" "By a deliberation, Sire, which I undertake to have the Senate pass, and whose effect Your Majesty will see at once." "You are sure of it?" "I answer for it, Sire." I convened the Senate on April 2, and that evening at seven o'clock I brought the Emperor Alexander the memorable deliberation, which I had had signed individually by every member of the assembly.

- Intent: he shows how the question of what the nation wanted was answered by a vote of the one body entitled to speak in its name, a vote he undertook to produce and delivered the same evening.
- Committee scenario: once, a sovereign asked the question the committee never asks: does the nation this decision is for actually want it? Talleyrand answered with a vote he could deliver by seven o'clock. In the committee room nobody asks even that much. The poll settles which design the room prefers, and no one stands to ask whether the unheard need either design, or whether the thing needs to be standardized at all. He knows from his own hand that a deliberation can be made to answer a question it was never built to ask, which is why the climax should name the question outright: the ceremonies cannot answer it because they were not designed to ask it. The form is his alone: a question once put by an emperor and answered by a procedure.
- Decisions: edit A6-7, G1, G4, G13 (prepares the Author's turn from the poll to the users).
- Drift risk: implying that committee polls are arranged in advance (G11), when the point is that a vote measures the voters and not the need; or letting anyone in the room voice the question, which must stay unasked there.

### A6-6: Repeated beat, the Author at his laptop (end of XXXVII. The New Ceremonies)

At "The Author opens his laptop. He begins to build."

- Original (Mémoires, tome I, Part III, his American exile in Philadelphia and New York, 1794-1796):

  > Mais rien n'embarrasse un homme qui, jeté loin de sa patrie, est dans une auberge ou dans un mauvais appartement: tout paraît plus difficile à celui qui est paisiblement assis sous son propre toit. Je profitai donc de la disposition où ma petite chambre mettait mon esprit, pour faire de la grande politique et arranger le monde. [...] je demandais un nouveau code général du droit des gens [...] lorsque, tout à coup, parut précisément un nouveau tarif pour les douanes, adopté par le congrès américain, sur la proposition de mon ami Hamilton. [...] «Vos économistes ont fait un beau rêve, me disait-il; c'est l'exagération chimérique de gens bien intentionnés. [...]»

- Translation (checked against Ledos de Beaufort, vol. I, Part III):

  > But nothing daunts a man who, cast far from his country, lives in an inn or a poor lodging; everything looks harder to the man peacefully seated under his own roof. So I made use of the turn my little room gave my mind, to practice grand politics and arrange the world. [...] I called for a new general code of the law of nations [...] when, all at once, there appeared a new customs tariff, adopted by the American Congress on the proposal of my friend Hamilton. [...] "Your economists have dreamed a beautiful dream," he would tell me; "it is the chimerical exaggeration of well-meaning people. [...]"

- Intent: he mocks his exile's habit of drafting universal codes in a small room, a habit cut short by a working tariff that a real legislature adopted and by Hamilton's verdict on the economists' beautiful dream.
- Committee scenario: every act ends with the Author back at his laptop, and Talleyrand's version is the man in temporary lodgings with the world to arrange. He knows the temptation: one more general code, one more paper for the room to poll. The Author does the other thing. He publishes a library that people can adopt or ignore on a Monday morning, the working measure rather than the beautiful dream, so that adoption decides whether it belongs in the standard. Building, in this act, means publishing where users decide instead of queueing for the next poll.
- Decisions: edit A6-11 ("He begins to build" is the moment the Author publishes), G13, G8 (the repeated beat retold through his own scenario), the protected line "The Author opens his laptop. He begins to build."
- Drift risk: casting the Author as the exile with a universal code, the very habit the passage mocks, or ending on another paper for the next mailing. The protected last line stays exactly as it is.
