# Act Three voice file: Franz Kafka

Voice card, passage map, quote ledger, and sources for Act Three of *The Room* (`parts/04-act-three.md`). Where this file disagrees with `AGENTS.md`, `AGENTS.md` wins.

<voice-card>

**Narrative stance**

- Person. First person only in the frame. The opening frame is the text under the act subtitle, before XIII. The closing frame is the narrator's own passages in XXII (what I did not finish, what I told Max, the legend) and the last line. Everything between is the Author's story in close third person, told as I told K.'s: from inside his perception, with no commentary and no knowledge he lacks. Beissner's description of the method is the standard: "Kafka lässt dem Erzähler keinen Raum neben oder über den Gestalten, keinen Abstand von dem Vorgang" (Kafka leaves the narrator no room beside or above the characters, no distance from the action).
- Tense. The Author's story is in the past tense, as the novel is ("Es war spät abends, als K. ankam"). The present tense belongs to the legend ("Vor dem Gesetz steht ein Türhüter") and may also hold the final laptop scene, where the book's motif lands in the present. The frame's "I" speaks in the present and closes on the past tense of his death.
- Addressee. No one. He never turns to the reader with "you" and never says "we" for the book's side of the argument. The one person he names as a confidant is Max, by first name, as in the notes he left him ("Liebster Max, meine letzte Bitte"). "You" appears only inside quoted speech, such as the doorkeeper's.
- His own works. The Castle is "my novel" (edit A3-1). The Trial is "my other novel" or "the novel before it." Before the Law is "the legend," his own word for it (diary, 13 December 1914: "Exegese der Legende"), or "the story the chaplain tells." He says "my K." at most once. He never writes "Kafka" outside the italic attribution line and the epigraph citations, and he never cites what critics say of his books.
- The other voices. By epithet, never by name: the Florentine, the philosopher. He has no reason to mention the voices of the later acts; if he must, the naturalist and the analyst. Others call him the clerk; he may call himself a clerk, in the first person.
- What he knows. Offices from the inside. He was an official of the Workers' Accident Insurance Institute in Prague from 1908 until he was pensioned off for illness in 1922, the year he wrote The Castle. The frame's "offices and courtrooms and insurance companies" is his own life and may stay.

**Sentence architecture**

- Long sentences of clauses joined by commas and semicolons, carrying stacked qualifications: zwar ... aber, freilich, allerdings, vielleicht, gewiß. In English: to be sure, of course, it is true, perhaps, certainly, after all, at least. A sentence asserts, concedes, qualifies, and keeps going.
- The self-correcting clause: an assertion withdrawn in mid-sentence for a stranger and more exact one.
- "As if" (wie wenn, als ob, wie absichtlich) attaches the uncanny to a concrete perception, never to an abstraction.
- Plain nouns in series, in the rhythm of fatigue: houses, windowpanes, snow, no one.
- Short declaratives for facts, then one long sentence for the reasoning that follows from them.
- Long paragraphs. Officials' speeches run on, benevolent and exhaustive, without paragraph breaks.
- Dialogue is formal and courteous on both sides. The narrator never mocks the officials' courtesy; he reports it.
- The narrator of the novel surfaces rarely, and then only in a parenthesis. One instance, in the description of the church tower of K.'s home town: "ein irdisches Gebäude - was können wir anderes bauen? - aber mit höherem Ziel als die niedrige Häusermenge" (an earthly building - what else can we build? - but with a higher aim than the low huddle of houses) (Das Schloß, chapter 1). The frame's "I" may sound like that: brief, parenthetical, often a question.

**Signature moves**

1. Exegesis of an official text. K. reads a document word by word and finds the burden moved onto himself. "es waren die drei Worte »wie Sie wissen« hinsichtlich seiner Aufnahme in den Dienst" - it was the three words "as you know" concerning his admission into service (Das Schloß, chapter 2). The mayor completes the reading: "die Beweislast dafür, daß Sie aufgenommen sind, ist Ihnen auferlegt" - the burden of proving that you have been taken on is laid on you (chapter 5).
2. The self-correcting sentence. "aber auch dieses Summen war keines, sondern war Gesang fernster, allerfernster Stimmen" - but this humming was no humming either, it was the singing of the farthest, the very farthest voices (chapter 2).
3. The benevolent official proves that the system cannot err. "denn Fehler kommen ja nicht vor, und selbst, wenn einmal ein Fehler vorkommt, wie in Ihrem Fall, wer darf denn endgültig sagen, daß es ein Fehler ist." - for errors do not happen, and even if an error does happen once, as in your case, who may say with finality that it is an error? (chapter 5)
4. Logic carried, deadpan, to the point of comedy. "und er muß bei der Vorzüglichkeit der Organisation den falschen Weg förmlich mit Eifer suchen, sonst findet er ihn nicht" - and given the excellence of the organization, a file must positively hunt for the wrong way with zeal, or it will never find it (chapter 5).
5. Two certainties held at once. "als sei er nun freilich freier als jemals [...] aber - diese Überzeugung war zumindest ebenso stark - als gäbe es gleichzeitig nichts Sinnloseres, nichts Verzweifelteres als diese Freiheit" - as if he were now, to be sure, freer than ever [...] but - and this conviction was at least as strong - as if at the same time there were nothing more senseless, nothing more desperate than this freedom (chapter 8).
6. The goal that keeps its distance, rendered as topography. "und wenn sie sich auch vom Schloß nicht entfernte, so kam sie ihm doch auch nicht näher" - and though it moved no farther from the castle, it came no nearer to it either (chapter 1).
7. Punishment without a verdict: nothing happens, and the nothing is the sentence. "Es geschah nichts. Keine Vorladung, keine Nachricht, kein Bericht, kein Besuch, nichts." - Nothing happened. No summons, no message, no report, no visit, nothing (chapter 15, "Amalias Strafe").
8. The comic particular inside the grave scene. The mayor's files roll out of the cupboard, "zwei große Aktenbündel [...], welche rund gebunden waren, so wie man Brennholz zu binden pflegt" - two big bundles of files, tied up round, the way one ties firewood - and the assistants shut the overstuffed cupboard by sitting on it until they can shout "Hurra!" (chapter 5). Brod reports that friends "laughed quite immoderately" when Kafka read the first chapter of The Trial aloud, and that he laughed so much he could not go on.

**Words**

- Use the plain vocabulary of offices: office, department, file, record, decree, summons, hearing, permission, the authorities, the gentlemen, in charge, competent. Use the hedges: to be sure, of course, it is true, perhaps, after all, at least, as if. Use physical particulars: snow, candle, cupboard, sleigh, badge, lanyard, coffee machine, projector, agenda. The users are the unseen, the act's word on the contents page.
- Avoid words that name the effect instead of showing it: absurd, Kafkaesque, nightmarish, labyrinthine, surreal, machine, bureaucratic, institutional, structural, systemic, ritual. Avoid psychological diagnosis: frozen, withdrawn into herself, wound, trauma. Ration what the book rations: "precise" and "precision" at most once in the part; "devastating" only in the protected line in XVI; "door" only in the doorkeeper motif; "It is not X. It is Y." and "not because X but because Y" at most twice each in the part.
- He never explains a scene, an image, or a quotation after showing it; never states the thesis in the narrator's voice; never quotes or paraphrases a critic; never names himself in the third person; never mocks anyone, official or delegate; never judges a person's psyche; never lets grief turn into lament. Of his deathbed scenes he wrote that the lament "bricht auch nicht etwa plötzlich ab wie wirkliche Klage, sondern verläuft schön und rein" (does not break off suddenly like a real lament, but runs its course clean and pure) (diary, 13 December 1914). Adorno's rule for reading him is the rule for writing him: "alles wörtlich nehmen, nichts durch Begriffe von oben zudecken" (take everything literally; cover nothing with concepts from above).

**Drift tests** (a yes is drift)

1. Does the paragraph say what a scene, image, or quotation means: an equation ("The reflector was this humming"), a "This is the nature of..." sentence, or a decoding of what it has just shown?
2. Outside the first-person frame, does it report anything the Author could not see, hear, or think at that moment, or look down on him from above?
3. Does it name "Kafka" outside the attribution line and the epigraph citations, call The Castle anything but "my novel," or quote or paraphrase a critic?
4. Does it put a word of judgment or atmosphere where a particular should be (absurd, Kafkaesque, cruel, terrifying, labyrinthine, devastating outside the protected line), or make anyone ridiculous?
5. Does it end on a maxim, a moral, or a rationed construction instead of an action, a perception, or someone's words?

**Narrative facts that bear on voice**

- First person, then K. Kafka wrote the first chapters of The Castle in the first person and, in the third chapter, switched to "K.", correcting the pages already written (franzkafka.de, "Das Schloss"). Hohoff describes the decision as one "aus der Ich-Erzählung nachträglich einen Text zu machen, in dem sich die Erzählerfigur nicht manifestiert" (to turn the first-person narration, after the fact, into a text in which the narrator figure does not show itself) (Hohoff 1986). The story's "I" was removed on purpose and must not show through in the Author's story. The frame's "I" is a different voice: the diarist's.
- When and where. He wrote it from late January to late August 1922, beginning at Spindlermühle in the Riesengebirge mountains, continuing in Prague, and breaking off at Planá (franzkafka.de). Around 11 September 1922 he wrote to Max that he had "die Schloßgeschichte offenbar für immer liegen lassen müssen" (apparently had to leave the castle story lying forever) (letter to Brod). "I write this in Prague" is true enough; "I began it in the mountains" is also true, and it answers "the philosopher in the mountains."
- Unfinished. The manuscript breaks off in the middle of a sentence, "aber was sie sagte" (but what she said) (Kleinwort 2013, citing the critical edition). Max's editions end cleanly, so the narrator should say that his manuscript breaks off, not that "the novel" does.
- The ending he told Max. K. was to die worn out by his struggle. Round his deathbed the villagers would gather, and word would come from the castle that he had no legal claim to live in the village but, in view of certain incidental circumstances, was nevertheless permitted to live and work there (Brod's afterword to the 1926 edition: "mit Rücksicht auf gewisse Nebenumstände"; in the Muir translation's "Additional Note": "taking certain auxiliary circumstances into account").
- The legend. Before the Law is the only part of The Trial he published in his lifetime (Selbstwehr, 1915; Ein Landarzt, 1920), and he called it "die Legende" (franzkafka.de, "Vor dem Gesetz"; diary, 13 December 1914).
- Burn everything. He asked Max to burn everything "restlos und ungelesen" (completely and unread), and Max had told him beforehand that he would not (Brod's afterword to Der Process, 1925).
- Comedy. Brod: friends "laughed quite immoderately" at the first chapter of The Trial, and Kafka "laughed so much that there were moments when he couldn't read any further" (Brod, Franz Kafka: A Biography, p. 178). Gravity in this voice is never solemn.

- ID: A3-1
- Heading: The Castle and the Committee (act subtitle; covers the opening frame before XIII)
- Original (Das Schloß, chapter 1, K.'s first daylight look at the castle, then his approach): Im ganzen entsprach das Schloß, wie es sich hier von der Ferne zeigte, K.s Erwartungen. Es war weder eine alte Ritterburg noch ein neuer Prunkbau, sondern eine ausgedehnte Anlage, die aus wenigen zweistöckigen, aber aus vielen eng aneinander stehenden niedrigen Bauten bestand; hätte man nicht gewußt, daß es ein Schloß sei, hätte man es für ein Städtchen halten können. [...] Aber im Näherkommen enttäuschte ihn das Schloß, es war doch nur ein recht elendes Städtchen, aus Dorfhäusern zusammengetragen, ausgezeichnet nur dadurch, daß vielleicht alles aus Stein gebaut war; aber der Anstrich war längst abgefallen, und der Stein schien abzubröckeln.
- Translation: On the whole the castle, as it showed itself here from a distance, matched K.'s expectations. It was neither an old knight's fortress nor a showy new mansion, but a sprawling complex made up of a few two-story buildings and a great many low ones standing close together; had one not known it was a castle, one might have taken it for a little town. [...] But as he came nearer, the castle disappointed him; it was only a rather wretched little town after all, gathered together out of village houses, distinguished only in that perhaps everything was built of stone; but the paint had long since flaked away, and the stone seemed to be crumbling.
- Intent: The power that rules the village turns out, looked at plainly, to be neither fortress nor palace but a spread of ordinary low buildings, and the nearer one comes, the less there is to see.
- Scenario: The frame's "I" looks at the committee the way K. first looks at the castle. It is not the Florentine's fortress or the philosopher's temple but a spread of low rooms close together (study groups, working groups, telecons, the reflector), which one could take for a little town; up close they are rooms with beige walls and adjustable lighting. The authority lives in no single building. Each small room asks whether a paper is correct and whether the room wants it, and none of them is a room for the unseen. The frame shows this from the road and does not say it.
- Decisions: edit A3-1, edit A3-2, G5, G7.
- Drift risk: The frame now states the thesis as a definition ("A castle that administers without governing... whose process processes but does not judge") and builds it on a "not as a principality or a temple but as something older" construction; it should show the castle from the road and stop. "They will let you know" closes the frame and spends the protected line of XVI before it lands, so cut it here. The frame's doors ("I knock on doors that do not open", "The doors are the same doors") are acceptable only as foreshadowing of the doorkeeper; keep them few.

- Original (Das Schloß, chapter 1, first paragraph): Es war spät abends, als K. ankam. Das Dorf lag in tiefem Schnee. Vom Schloßberg war nichts zu sehen, Nebel und Finsternis umgaben ihn, auch nicht der schwächste Lichtschein deutete das große Schloß an. Lange stand K. auf der Holzbrücke, die von der Landstraße zum Dorf führte, und blickte in die scheinbare Leere empor.
- Translation: It was late in the evening when K. arrived. The village lay in deep snow. Nothing could be seen of the castle hill; fog and darkness surrounded it, and not even the faintest glimmer of light hinted at the great castle. For a long time K. stood on the wooden bridge that led from the main road to the village and gazed up into the seeming emptiness.
- Intent: K. arrives at night in a place whose ruling power is present but invisible, and stands looking up into an emptiness that only seems empty.
- Scenario: The Author arrives in the meeting city in the evening; the venue is somewhere past the last roundabout; he stands at the taxi stand looking up into what seems a void (the section already transforms "blickte in die scheinbare Leere empor"). His paper's number tells nothing; the agenda is "provisional"; his slot is fifteen minutes on Wednesday, against the established paper's forty-five. The history that will decide his paper (prior polls, reflector threads, hallway talk) is present and unseen. He arrives believing the paper is its own credential. Where it reads naturally, the credential becomes deployment and the users who depend on the design (G4), and he still wants his design to win (G13).
- Decisions: edit A3-1, edit A3-2, G4, G5, G6, G13; protected: "anything discussed within them has already been decided elsewhere".
- Drift risk: "a history that existed the way the castle exists in Kafka's novel" names the narrator in the third person and explains the epigraph's image (drift tests 1 and 3); cut the simile and let the history stay unseen. The Author's suspicion that the guidelines' contradictions might be intentional is good close third person and anticipates K. reading Klamm's letter (A3-5); keep it.

- ID: A3-3
- Heading: XIV. The Telephone
- Original (Das Schloß, chapter 2, K. telephones the castle from the Bridge Inn): Aus der Hörmuschel kam ein Summen, wie K. es sonst beim Telefonieren nie gehört hatte. Es war, wie wenn sich aus dem Summen zahlloser kindlicher Stimmen - aber auch dieses Summen war keines, sondern war Gesang fernster, allerfernster Stimmen -, wie wenn sich aus diesem Summen in einer geradezu unmöglichen Weise eine einzige hohe, aber starke Stimme bilde, die an das Ohr schlug, so, wie wenn sie fordere, tiefer einzudringen als nur in das armselige Gehör. Supporting, chapter 5, the mayor: Nun ist aber dieses Rauschen und dieser Gesang das einzig Richtige und Vertrauenswerte, was uns die hiesigen Telefone übermitteln, alles andere ist trügerisch.
- Translation: Out of the receiver came a humming such as K. had never heard on the telephone before. It was as if, out of the humming of countless childish voices - but this humming was no humming either, it was the singing of the farthest, the very farthest voices - as if out of this humming, in a way that was frankly impossible, a single high but strong voice were forming that beat against the ear as if demanding to go deeper than into mere paltry hearing. Supporting: But this rushing and this singing is the only right and trustworthy thing the telephones here transmit to us; everything else is deceptive.
- Intent: The castle's ceaseless internal traffic reaches the village only as an inhuman hum that seems to gather into one commanding voice, and the mayor says the hum is the one true thing the line conveys.
- Scenario: The reflector, read for three months: hundreds of voices on matters the Author half understands, which now and then gather, impossibly, into a single high voice, the names that recur. The hum is the only reliable thing it tells him: that the committee is busy talking to itself. The four replies to his paper are not answers. Two ask "What is the deployment experience?" and "Has this been through a study group?", and they are Schwarzer's demand for the count's permission (chapter 1). He has deployment data and users who depend on the design, and it satisfies nothing, because what is asked for is standing. The nearest the room comes to asking about users is asked as a permit check and filed.
- Decisions: edit A3-2, G4, G5, G6, G7, G10; protected: the lanyard colors.
- Drift risk: "The reflector was this humming." and "These questions were not requests for information. They were requests for credentials." equate and decode (drift test 1), and the second is an "It is not X. It is Y."; show the hum, the four replies, and Schwarzer's sentence, and let the reader join them. Do not import the mayor's next sentences, in which a tired official's answer is "nichts [...] als Scherz" (nothing but a joke); on the committee's side that would mock delegates (G10).

- Heading: XV. The Village Street That Curves Away
- Original (Das Schloß, chapter 1, K.'s first walk toward the castle): So ging er wieder vorwärts, aber es war ein langer Weg. Die Straße nämlich, die Hauptstraße des Dorfes, führte nicht zum Schloßberg, sie führte nur nahe heran, dann aber, wie absichtlich, bog sie ab, und wenn sie sich auch vom Schloß nicht entfernte, so kam sie ihm doch auch nicht näher. Immer erwartete K., daß nun endlich die Straße zum Schloß einlenken müsse und nur, weil er es erwartete, ging er weiter; offenbar infolge seiner Müdigkeit zögerte er, die Straße zu verlassen, auch staunte er über die Länge des Dorfes, das kein Ende nahm, immer wieder die kleinen Häuschen und vereisten Fensterscheiben und Schnee und Menschenleere [...]
- Translation: So he went on again, but it was a long way. For the street, the village's main street, did not lead to the castle hill; it only led close to it, and then, as if on purpose, it turned aside, and though it moved no farther from the castle, it came no nearer to it either. K. kept expecting that now at last the street must turn toward the castle, and only because he expected it did he go on; plainly it was his tiredness that made him hesitate to leave the street, and he was amazed, too, at the length of the village, which had no end, again and again the little houses and the iced-over windowpanes and snow and no one anywhere [...]
- Intent: The road that seems to lead to the castle runs beside it at a fixed distance; K. goes on only because he expects it to turn, and the village has no end.
- Scenario: The published sequence (mailing, study group, working group, plenary) appears to lead from paper to standard, and each step keeps the Author at the same distance: the study group's full agenda and "the following cycle"; the working group's "after the current work"; SD-4's own order of business, in which chairs take first the direction group's priorities, the work aimed at the next standard, and work that can reach plenary at this meeting, and "After that they get to things on a best-effort basis as time allows." He walks on, R2, R3, because he expects the road to turn. Each step asks whether the paper fits the current work and whether the room wants it; the road was laid for the papers already on it.
- Decisions: edit A3-2, G5, G6, G7, G9, G12.
- Drift risk: "The committee's process is this street." and the closing paragraph that explains the structure ("the favoritism is not deliberate. It is structural.") fail drift tests 1 and 5; walk the Author along the street instead. Fix G9: mailings are monthly, larger around meetings, on the order of one or two hundred papers, not "three hundred of them every cycle". The walk spans several cycles in the present tense while XIII to XIX cover one week; keep it in the past tense, as the Author's later walk or his foreknowledge, so it does not collide with XIX's "chair of the study group that had forwarded the Author's paper".

- ID: A3-5
- Heading: XVI. The Letter
- Original (Das Schloß, chapter 2, K. rereads Klamm's letter by candlelight in his attic room): Er war nicht einheitlich, es gab Stellen, wo mit ihm wie mit einem Freien gesprochen wurde, dessen eigenen Willen man anerkennt, so war die Überschrift, so war die Stelle, die seine Wünsche betraf. Es gab aber wieder Stellen, wo er offen oder versteckt als ein kleiner, vom Sitz jenes Vorstandes kaum bemerkbarer Arbeiter behandelt wurde [...]. Das waren zweifellos Widersprüche, sie waren so sichtbar, daß sie beabsichtigt sein mußten. Den einer solchen Behörde gegenüber wahnwitzigen Gedanken, daß hier Unentschlossenheit mitgewirkt habe, streifte K. kaum.
- Translation: It was not all of one piece. There were places where he was spoken to as a free man whose own will is recognized; the salutation was like that, and so was the place that concerned his wishes. But there were other places where he was treated, openly or covertly, as a minor worker, barely noticeable from the seat of that department head [...]. These were contradictions beyond doubt, so plain that they had to be intended. The thought that indecision had played a part here, a mad thought where such an authority was concerned, barely crossed K.'s mind.
- Intent: K. reads the official letter word for word and finds that it addresses him as a free man and as a petty worker at once; he dismisses as madness the thought that the authority simply had not decided.
- Scenario: The working group chair's email, read three times: "we appreciate your contribution" beside "when the schedule permits" and the advice to attend for "context". The courtesy is the devastation, and one cannot appeal a compliment (both protected). He asks how to get scheduled and is told "We will let you know" (protected). Like K., the Author refuses the one explanation he cannot bear, that no one has decided anything; XX returns to it (edit A3-3). The letter answers whether the room has time; it never asks whether anyone needs the paper. K.'s close reading of "the three words 'as you know'" is the model for the Author's three readings.
- Decisions: edit A3-2, edit A3-3, G6, G7; protected: "It was courteous and devastating, and the courtesy was the devastation", "One cannot object to being thanked. One cannot appeal a compliment.", "We will let you know".
- Drift risk: "This is the nature of institutional communication. It is designed to satisfy the reading of anyone who..." explains the scene and asserts a design (drift tests 1 and 5); cut it. "The email was not a refusal. It was something worse than a refusal." spends one of the part's two "It is not X. It is Y." The sleigh passage (chapter 8) is quoted here and again in XIX's epigraph and body; let it land once, in XIX.

- Original (Das Schloß, chapter 6, the landlady shows K. her keepsakes): »[...] ich dagegen, die nur dreimal bei Klamm war - später ließ er mich nicht mehr rufen, ich weiß nicht, warum -, habe doch wie in Vorahnung der Kürze meiner Zeit diese Andenken mitgebracht. - Translation: "[...] whereas I, who went to Klamm only three times - later he no longer sent for me, I don't know why - brought these keepsakes away with me, as if I had foreseen how short my time would be. "Over twenty years," said the landlady. "Well over twenty years."
- Intent: The landlady has arranged her whole life around three summonses from an official who then stopped sending for her, for no reason she knows, and she explains the castle to the newcomer as only someone who was once let in can.
- Scenario: The Patron at the hotel bar. Her paper was heard once (twelve minutes, three speakers, "the working group has already chosen a direction") and never again, and she does not know why; she kept coming. Her advice ends in the protected "death of the author's will", which is K.'s own fear in chapter 2: "K. wußte, daß nicht mit wirklichem Zwang gedroht war, [...] aber die Gewalt der entmutigenden Umgebung, der Gewöhnung an Enttäuschungen, die Gewalt der unmerklichen Einflüsse jedes Augenblicks, die fürchtete er allerdings" (K. knew that no real compulsion was threatened [...] but the force of discouraging surroundings, of growing used to disappointment, the force of the imperceptible influences of every moment, those he did fear). - Decisions: edit A3-7, G4, G6, G7, G11; protected: "You are being processed. You are not being evaluated.", "the death of the author's will"; book boundary: no sexual-coercion analogies.

Keep the analogy on the three summonses, the silence after them, and the keepsakes, never on the liaison, and recast that sentence (confidence: high, the book bars sexual-coercion analogies). "Eighteen years" contradicts the source ("well over twenty years") and blurs the protected eighteen months of XXII. - Translation: "[...] When a matter has been weighed for a very long time, it can happen, even before the weighing is finished, that suddenly, like lightning, at some unforeseeable place that cannot be found again afterward, a settlement comes forth that closes the matter, quite correctly as a rule, but arbitrarily all the same. It is as if the official apparatus could no longer bear the tension, the years of irritation by the same matter, trifling perhaps in itself, and had made the decision out of itself, without the officials' help. [...] The only troubling thing about these decisions is that, as is usually the way of things, one learns of them too late, and so in the meantime goes on passionately debating matters long since decided. [...]"
- Intent: After years of deliberation the apparatus sometimes closes a case by itself, suddenly, from a place no one can find afterward, usually correctly but arbitrarily, while people go on debating what was decided long ago.
- Scenario: Eighteen months on, the established facility has shipped and its correction papers have begun, and the Author's paper gets a new room, a new chair, twelve people, forty-five minutes, and a poll that takes thirty seconds and is unanimous for "exploring this direction" (protected). No one can say who decided, or when; it is as if the room could no longer bear the paper that kept coming back, as the Patron foretold. In this act the eventual poll is not a judgment but an old irritation discharged, recording what was settled elsewhere, long before (protected: "anything discussed within them has already been decided elsewhere"). At last the room wants the paper; nobody asks whether the unseen needed either facility. The ending Brod reported shares the mayor's word: K. would be permitted to live and work there in view of certain incidental circumstances, and "Nebenumstände" is what the mayor blames for confusing the summons ("nur Nebenumstände haben verwirrend eingegriffen", chapter 5).
- Decisions: G8 (repeated beat), G1, G13; protected: the eighteen-month unanimous poll and the ending Brod reported; "anything discussed within them has already been decided elsewhere".
- Drift risk: Keep the protected facts exact (eighteen months, unanimous, thirty seconds) and keep both halves of Brod's report: no legal claim, and yet permission. Do not explain the lightning after the scene (drift test 1). This is not a second unasked-question moment (G1); the question stays in XVIII. Do not resolve the Author's arc (G13).
