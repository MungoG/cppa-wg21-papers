## ABI: What It Is and Why Proposals Die On It

Plain meaning for a newcomer (synthesized from source usage; see flag): ABI is about whether already-compiled programs keep running without being recompiled. A library type's memory layout and calling details get baked into every binary that uses it. Change them and you break those binaries. The sources use ABI exactly this way but never spell out the acronym.

Source facts:

- Link compatibility is the default. SD-10's compatibility principle treats full link compatibility with C and prior C++ as the baseline, and "an ABI break is a case-by-case exception with documented reasons" (Part 3, §3.2).
- A shipped component cannot be taken back. "A shipped library component cannot be unshipped" (Part 1, §1.3). Only one feature has ever been fully removed from the standard, and it took years (Part 1, §1.3).
- That is why proposals die on ABI grounds: a worse-but-shipped type cannot be swapped for a faster one without breaking existing binaries, so the committee keeps the slower one.

Concrete, source-named examples a newcomer can grasp:

- `std::regex` has been slower than RE2, PCRE, and Boost.Regex for over a decade. It is ABI-locked and cannot be repaired without breaking binaries (Part 1, §1.3; Part 3, §3.4).
- `std::unordered_map` is far slower than open-addressing tables because the standard mandates node-based separate chaining in the interface itself, which makes it structurally unfixable (Part 3, §3.4).
- `std::regex`, `std::unordered_map`, and `std::error_code` are all named as frozen components the market has already routed around (Part 3, §3.4).

Flag: the four sources never define "ABI" or "Application Binary Interface" in plain words. The writer must supply the newcomer definition (layout and calling convention baked into binaries).

## Backward Compatibility: The Weight of Existing Code

- The compatibility lens. SD-10 asks whether the feature keeps link compatibility with C and prior C++, whether 100% source compatibility is available, and whether the feature avoids splitting the standard library (Part 3, §3.2).
- No bifurcation. Two competing `vector` types or two `span` types break composition at function signatures, so the standard refuses to carry a second "safe" copy of a vocabulary type (Part 3, §3.1-§3.2). This is a clean, concrete example for newcomers.
- What governs it on the library side. SD-8 (Standard Library Compatibility) codifies what may change in the library without breaking compatibility, which is to say what may not change (Part 1, §1.3).
- The irreversibility framing. The ecosystem fixes a mistake in its next release. The standard ships a mistake for decades (Part 3, §3.4).

Flag: the "40 years of existing code" figure is the chapter's framing, not a source fact. The sources anchor backward compatibility to the "1990 founding principle" (Part 3, §3.4) and to prior standards (C++98 onward), not to a 40-year number.

## The Zero-Overhead Principle

- The statement, verbatim from SD-10 as rendered in the source: "What you do not use, you do not pay for. What you do use is as efficient as the hand-written equivalent" (Part 3, §3.2). The prompt's phrasing "you don't pay for what you don't use" matches this.
- It is a principle, not a rule. SD-10 is "design defaults with explicit exceptions." A principle is "a default you must argue past in writing," not a wall, and EWG may override it for a documented reason (Part 3, §3.1-§3.2).
- The paired principle. SD-10 also says keep C's ability to touch hardware directly, control data layout, and map primitive operations one-to-one to the machine, leaving no room for a language below C++ other than assembler (Part 3, §3.2). Same performance worldview as zero-overhead.
- How it shapes proposals. A feature that taxes users who never touch it fails this lens, and the author is expected to answer the objection inside the paper before the floor raises it (Part 3, §3.2).

## "Standardize Existing Practice"

- The founding principle. In 1990 the committee held that the standard would be based on existing practice (Part 3, §3.4). The source labels this Use-Case-First.
- The stated drift. One practitioner, quoted in the source, says the committee "has taken on the role of innovator and is pushing the industry rather than adopting external advancements" (Part 3, §3.4). The source labels the opposite Framework-First.
- Why it is the lowest-risk path. Because the standard cannot easily correct itself, a component imposed on every user clears a higher bar than an ecosystem library, since the market's self-correction does not reach inside the standard (Part 3, §3.4).
- Worked examples of existing practice becoming standard: `{fmt}` before `std::format`, range-v3 before `std::ranges`, and the Boost components that became C++11 (Part 3, §3.4). The STL is the archetype: Stepanov and Lee implemented it at HP Labs before presenting it, and it shipped largely as proposed in C++98 (Part 3, §3.6).
- The staged, lower-risk pattern. Ship independently useful primitives first and measure adoption before committing to a monolith (Part 7, §7.2). Contracts is a worked case: it succeeded on its fourth attempt with a minimum-viable design that shipped the agreed core and deferred the contested parts (Part 3, §3.6).
- The counter-fact. WG21 "does not have any requirement on implementation experience to adopt a proposal" (P2274R0, Ballman 2020, quoted in Part 7, §7.1). Existing practice is preferred, not required.

Flag on the Direction Group: the sources present the Direction Group as a by-invitation body (Part 1, §1.5) that endorses directions and priorities, for example profiles in P2759R1 (Part 7, §7.1). They do not state that the Direction Group's "strongest preference" is standardizing existing practice. That preference is sourced to the 1990 founding principle, not attributed to the Direction Group. P0939 ("Direction for ISO C++") is the Direction Group paper, but the sources cite it only for its "bunch of volunteers" and "clever people drawn to clever solutions" characterization (Part 7, §7.7). Confirm the existing-practice preference directly in P0939.

## The Prague ABI Vote (2020)

Flag, important: the four authorized sources do not describe a Prague ABI vote. They give no date, no outcome, and name no Prague ABI papers.

- The only Prague reference in these files: C++20 Contracts (P0542R5) were "adopted at Cologne and removed at Prague," with no production deployment reported either way (Part 7, §7.1). That is about Contracts, not ABI.
- The "implementers' de facto veto formalized into explicit policy," the "Feb 2020" date, and any named Prague ABI papers are not present in any of the four files. The date in the prompt is user-supplied, not source-verified.
- Adjacent facts the sources do support: ABI breaks are strongly disfavored by policy, because SD-10 sets full link compatibility as the default and an ABI break as a documented case-by-case exception (Part 3, §3.2), and ABI shields shipped components from replacement (Part 3, §3.4).
