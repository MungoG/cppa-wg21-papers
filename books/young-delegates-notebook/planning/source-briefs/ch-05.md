# Chapter 5 Source Brief: How WG21 Is Structured

Condensed fact brief for the writer. Neutral structural facts only, drawn from `wg21-bible/part-1`, the train-model paper (P4131R0), and `wg21-milieu`. Topics the sources do not cover are flagged at the end.

## Where WG21 Sits (the ISO chain)

- ISO and IEC jointly own JTC1, the committee for information technology. JTC1 contains SC22, the subcommittee for programming languages. SC22 contains WG21, the working group for C++.
- A working group, in the Directives' words, "operates by consensus, reports and gives recommendations, if any, to its parent committee through a Convenor." WG21 decisions are recommendations, not law.
- A standard becomes official through National Body ballots at the SC22 level: Committee Draft (CD), then Draft International Standard (DIS, a 3-month enquiry ballot needing a two-thirds majority of P-member national bodies), then Final Draft International Standard (FDIS, an 8-week approval vote, editorial comments only). One country, one vote.
- ISO regulates nothing below the working-group boundary. The 2023 Directives say only "Working groups may establish subgroups." No required chair, term, or membership rule for subgroups.
- Scale: roughly 250 to 350 active participants from 24 to 31 nations; a single in-person meeting draws around 200 delegates. Sofia 2025 seated 21 nations. The committee grew from about 50 members in the 1990s. (Sources differ on the headline number: `part-1` says "around two hundred" attend; `wg21-milieu` says "approximately 350 active delegates." Treat 250-350 as the active-participant range, ~200 as typical meeting attendance.)

## The Four Permanent Rooms (EWG, LEWG, CWG, LWG)

- Four permanent design and wording groups. Two decide direction, two settle text.
- EWG (Evolution Working Group): core-language design and direction.
- LEWG (Library Evolution Working Group): library design and direction.
- CWG (Core Working Group): core-language wording, specification-quality text.
- LWG (Library Working Group): library wording.
- Flow within a feature: the design group decides direction, the matching wording group settles precise text, then plenary ratifies that text into the working draft.
- Note: the source files use the acronyms and describe each group's function but do not spell out the full names. The expansions above are standard committee usage (high confidence correct), not verbatim from the sources.

## Study Groups (SG1 through ~SG23)

- Study groups incubate raw ideas before a design group sees them. Numbered SG1 through SG23.
- About 13 study groups are active at a time. Total active subgroup count is roughly 22 to 23.
- Named in the sources: SG10 (feature-test macros; owns SD-6 / SD-FeatureTest) and SG22 (C and C++ liaison; coordinates with WG14, the C committee, through paired straw polls and quorum rules per P2274R0).
- SD-3 governs study-group organization. The convener creates and disbands study groups.
- Graduation path: study group, then design group (EWG or LEWG), then wording group (CWG or LWG), then plenary.
- Flag: individual study-group topics beyond SG10 and SG22 are not enumerated in the sources.

## The Train Model (three-year cadence)

- Herb Sutter proposed the three-year release cadence at Bloomington, Indiana, in August 2011 (minutes N3316). The committee adopted it.
- Codified in P1000 (Sutter), revised across R2 through R6.
- Core claim, P1000R2: "ship what's ready." Safety valve, P1000R6: "If we see a feature is not ready yet, we pull it back out to let it bake more."
- Original rationale (N3316): kill "feature creep - the mentality that if a feature does not get in now it never will," by replacing "now or never" pressure with a known next train.
- On-time releases under the model: C++14 (2014), C++17 (2017), C++20 (2020), C++23 (2023). Then C++26, with C++29 next.
- Before the train model: C++98 (1998), C++03 (2003, a bug-fix release), then a nine-year gap to C++11 (2011).
- Safety-valve example: contracts pulled from C++20 at Cologne, July 2019 (P1823R0), plenary vote 68 in favor, 0 against, 4 abstaining. This is a pre-ship pull, the mechanism the model intends.
- Paper volume feeding the cadence: 300 to 500 papers per year.

## Standing Documents (SD-3 through SD-10)

- Eight active standing documents. The set defines what the committee governs.
- SD-3: study-group organization.
- SD-4: practices and procedures ("WG21 Practices and Procedures: The How We Work Cheat Sheet"). The only standing document that creates governance rules.
- SD-5: meeting information and logistics.
- SD-6 / SD-FeatureTest: feature-test macros, owned by SG10.
- SD-7: mailing procedures and how to write papers.
- SD-8: standard-library compatibility (what may change without breaking).
- SD-9: Library Evolution policies.
- SD-10: Language Evolution principles.
- SD-1 (document register) and SD-2 (membership) are retired.
- Owners: the convener holds SD-3, SD-4, SD-5; LEWG holds SD-8 and SD-9; EWG holds SD-10.
- New standing documents are created by plenary consensus, usually unanimous consent. Owners make editorial updates. No standing document carries a sunset clause or mandatory periodic review.
- SD-4's decision model: a 5-point straw-poll scale (Strongly Favor, Weakly Favor, Neutral, Weakly Against, Strongly Against). Consensus means "absence of sustained opposition." The 2:1 guideline: a proposal "normally advances if there are more than twice as many in favor as against," as determined by the subgroup chair.

## The Convener

- The single office ISO governs inside WG21. SC22 appoints the convener for three-year terms; the National Body confirms the appointment.
- Powers: appoints all subgroup chairs, creates and disbands study groups, controls the plenary, and sets the meeting schedule.
- Chairs are appointed by the convener and have no fixed term (SD-4). Direction Group membership is by invitation.
- Tenure: one occupant held the role for over two decades, with a transition in 2026. The 2026 convener is the first from outside the United States since 1990.
- Analysis paper on the office's influence: P4130R0 (an advocacy/analysis paper, not a neutral committee document; note this if cited).

## Chair Discretion

- Each room is run by a chair. The chair controls scheduling (which papers reach the agenda, in what order, for how long), poll wording, and the consensus call.
- Consensus has no numeric threshold or formula in SD-4; the chair reads the room. The 2:1 guideline is explicitly left to "the subgroup chair."
- Chairs have no fixed terms. The only path to remove a chair runs to the convener who appointed them.

## The Direction Group (P0939)

- Sets committee priorities and design philosophy. Membership is by invitation, not election.
- Publishes the "Direction for ISO C++" papers: P0939 (Stroustrup, Sutter, Vandevoorde) and the P2000 series (Stroustrup, Hinnant, Orr, Vandevoorde, Wong).
- Examples of its role in the sources: assessed the pandemic cycle's impact (P2000R4), weighed in on contracts timing (P0939R4), endorsed Profiles (P2759R1).

## How Papers Move and Get Prioritized

- Pipeline: study group, then design group (EWG/LEWG), then wording group (CWG/LWG), then plenary ratification into the working draft.
- A paper is "forwarded" from one group to the next by a poll in the sending group.
- Two gates can kill a paper: the schedule and the poll. Most die for lack of a recorded vote rather than because of one. An unsympathetic chair need not oppose anything; the paper simply never reaches the agenda.
- Prioritization: features targeting the next International Standard and items aligned with Direction Group priorities tend to get agenda time. (Synthesis. The sources establish that chairs control scheduling and the Direction Group sets priorities, but do not state a single formal prioritization rule. Medium confidence.)

## Parallel Sessions and the Schedule

- Full-week meetings happen three times a year. Six or seven subgroups run in parallel rooms (six parallel tracks is the figure cited).
- A delegate can sit in only one of the simultaneous rooms, so schedule conflicts across the ~22-23 subgroups are unavoidable.
- Plenary reconvenes the whole committee to vote on all topics after the parallel sessions.

## Feature Removal

- Shipped library components effectively cannot be unshipped; binary and ABI compatibility block repair. Example: the slow `std::regex` has been a known defect for over a decade and cannot be fixed without breaking binaries. SD-8 governs what may change without breaking compatibility.
- Only one feature has been fully removed in the committee's history, and it took years. (The source does not name the feature.)
- Pre-ship pulls are different from removing shipped features. Contracts were pulled from C++20 (2019). Trivial relocation was removed before C++26 shipped.

## The Implementer Veto and the Volunteer Nature

- WG21 is a volunteer body that operates by consensus and issues recommendations. It cannot compel anyone to do work.
- The standard has no mechanism to force implementation. If GCC, Clang, or MSVC decline to implement a feature, the standard cannot compel them.
- Daveed Vandevoorde, a senior EDG implementer, predicted in P3590R0 that major implementations will "opt not to be standard compliant for the better part of a decade," choosing which parts to implement.
- Eighteen implementers from GCC, Clang, MSVC, EDG, libc++, and libstdc++ wrote P3962R0, stating that features "accumulate faster than they can be fully implemented" and asking the committee to slow the pace.
- Scheduling as a quiet veto: a deprioritized paper never reaches the agenda, so it never reaches a vote. Examples cited: stackful coroutines remain outside the working draft after twelve years; the Networking TS (based on Boost.Asio) was set aside.
