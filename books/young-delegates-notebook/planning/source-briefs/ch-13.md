# Chapter 13 Source Brief: Common Mistakes

## 1. Showing up without announcing

- **The mistake.** Arriving with no notice and expecting a seat. part-8 names it: "Showing up with no notice and expecting a seat."
- **Why it is a mistake.** Access runs through the convener. Members register ahead. Guests give the convener one week's notice. New attendees are expected at the Sunday 6pm orientation, which sets the week's logistics and norms.
- **Correct behavior.** Notify the convener a week ahead and attend the Sunday orientation. Guests take full part in subgroup work and straw polls. They do not vote in plenary.

## 2. Proposing core language features too early

- **The mistake.** Bringing a core language proposal before you have watched how the room works.
- **Why it is a mistake.** Core changes run through EWG, the design group for the language. Library proposals go to LEWG. A proposal first incubates in a study group, earns design-group direction, goes to a wording group, then to plenary. Several gates.
- **Correct behavior.** Learn how EWG evaluates a design first. Socialize the idea in hallways and the study group before a cold presentation (Codex 4.1: getting on the agenda beats winning the poll). Bring evidence, not just an idea (Codex 4.6: "Arrive with running code").

## 3. Writing an "idea only" paper

- **The mistake.** A paper that states an idea with no worked examples, no alternatives weighed, no implementation evidence.
- **Why it is a mistake.** A proposal with no paper does not exist, but a thin paper wastes the time it claims. No formal rule requires implementation experience to adopt a proposal (P2274R0, Ballman 2020), so the bar is cultural. Strong papers bring evidence anyway.
- **Correct behavior.** Supply examples, the alternatives you considered, and implementation or field evidence (Codex 4.6: from day one, even on a first paper).

## 4. Voting on things you haven't followed

- **The mistake.** Casting a vote on a poll you never followed, moving with the room instead.
- **Why it is a mistake.** The committee publishes 300 to 500 papers a year, and the median delegate has an informed opinion on only about one plenary poll in ten. A passive vote still counts under the consensus model.
- **Correct behavior.** Read in proportion to where you vote. Where you have not followed a topic, the honest position is Neutral or abstain, not voting with the room. Hearing a summary is not being familiar.

## 5. Quoting reflectors or meeting notes publicly

- **The mistake.** Pasting a reflector post, wiki text, or meeting record into a blog post or paper. Live-posting from a session.
- **Why it is a mistake.** SD-4 designates meeting records, wikis, and reflector posts as protected materials. Quoting them publicly breaks committee confidentiality.
- **Correct behavior.** Paraphrase committee discussion. Two narrow exceptions let you quote: poll questions and numeric results, freely and with no permission; a specific person's words or position, only with that person's prior consent. The meeting guidelines (N4916) bar live tweeting, blogging, photos, and video from sessions, and recording is not permitted.

## 6. Not raising concerns early enough

- **The mistake.** Surfacing an objection for the first time at closing plenary. part-8: it "earns it nothing."
- **Why it is a mistake.** Objections belong on record where the work happens, in the subgroup. SD-4 gives no weight in plenary to objections not escalated by 5pm the evening before closing plenary.
- **Correct behavior.** Raise a "cannot live with" objection early in the subgroup. Bring a paper, because a concern with no paper carries little weight. Meet the 5pm deadline, then follow SD-4's escalation path up from the subgroup chair if it stays unresolved.

## 7. Expecting majority rule

- **The mistake.** Assuming the committee counts hands and the larger number wins.
- **Why it is a mistake.** The decision model is consensus, defined by ISO as "the absence of sustained opposition to substantial issues by any important part of the concerned interests." It is neither majority rule nor a minority veto.
- **Correct behavior.** Read a poll as a measure of consensus, not a headcount. Subgroup straw polls use a five-point scale (Strongly Favor, Weakly Favor, Neutral, Weakly Against, Strongly Against), and a proposal normally advances when those in favor outnumber those against by more than two to one (the 2:1 guideline in SD-4). Plenary defaults to unanimous consent and, on objection, becomes a three-way Favor, Against, Neutral poll. A well-founded objection from an important part of the concerned interests can block advancement even when a majority is in favor.

## 8. Talking too much at your first meetings

- **The mistake.** Speaking often at your first meetings before you understand the room.
- **Why it is a mistake.** Newcomers absorb the group's norms and vocabulary over time. Early meetings are for learning how the pipeline and the rooms actually work.
- **Correct behavior.** Listen first, and contribute where you add something specific: a paper review, scribing, a worked example. Offer service over branding (Codex 4.5). Record what you consider normal before your second meeting so you can check yourself later (Codex 4.8).

## 9. Misreading "encouragement" as approval

- **The mistake.** Hearing "we would like to see more" or "come back with a revision" and treating it as "this will ship."
- **Why it is a mistake.** A favorable straw poll in a study or design group is direction to keep going, not adoption. Revisions (R0, R1, R2) are the normal path. A paper returning with a higher revision is expected, not a victory.
- **Correct behavior.** Treat early favorable signals as "keep going." Confirm what was polled and at which stage before reading it as approval. Adoption comes later, in plenary, after wording review.

## 10. Ignoring subgroup direction

- **The mistake.** Getting directional guidance from EWG or LEWG, then ignoring it in the next revision.
- **Why it is a mistake.** Design groups exist to set direction, EWG for the language and LEWG for the library. Consensus means the absence of sustained opposition. Work against the direction the subgroup set and you keep that opposition alive, so the proposal stalls.
- **Correct behavior.** Follow the subgroup's direction. If you disagree, reopen it early in that same subgroup, with a paper, rather than routing around it.

## 11. Declaring victory at design approval

- **The mistake.** Treating a design-group "approved" as the finish line.
- **Why it is a mistake.** Design approval is an early milestone. After it, the proposal still needs specification-quality text from a wording group (CWG for the language, LWG for the library), then plenary adoption into the working draft, then national-body balloting.
- **Correct behavior.** Budget for the wording and ballot stages. The DIS (Draft International Standard) is a three-month enquiry ballot needing a two-thirds majority of P-member national bodies. The FDIS (Final Draft International Standard) is an eight-week approval vote on editorial comments only. A ballot comment requesting a feature absent from the balloted document is out of scope.
