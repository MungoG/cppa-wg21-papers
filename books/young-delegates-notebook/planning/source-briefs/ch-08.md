# Chapter 8 Source Brief: How Decisions Get Made

Scope: condensed, neutral facts for a writer covering polls, consensus, voting, and ballots. Procedural only. No reform framing.

## The straw poll and the five-point scale

- WG21 polls at two levels: subgroup and plenary (d4129).
- A subgroup straw poll uses a five-point scale. Modern wording: Strongly Favor (SF), Weakly Favor (WF), Neutral (N), Weakly Against (WA), Strongly Against (SA). Older minutes sometimes record the weak positions as plain Favor (F) and Against (A); several published polls show the SF/F/N/A/SA form. The writer should teach the modern SF/WF/N/WA/SA form and may note the older variant.
- In a subgroup, everyone present may vote. This is the basis for the "guests can vote in subgroups" fact.
- The chair reads the result and decides whether consensus exists. There is no fixed numeric threshold; the chair has discretion (P1338R1: the chair decides what counts as consensus within their subgroup).
- The straw poll follows a presentation and a question-and-answer session. The vote reflects the discussion, not only the written paper.
- Origin worth quoting: the committee's 1991 founding meeting established that straw votes "are designed to give a good indication of how formal votes will turn out" and therefore "follow the same rules as formal votes" (N0013).

## What each position means: Neutral versus abstaining

- Neutral is an informed position. The voter understands the proposal, has evaluated it, and is actively undecided. Neutral votes should be rare.
- Abstaining means not voting at all. It signals the voter does not wish to express an opinion, from either lack of familiarity or choice.
- Practical guidance for the reader (Reform Codex, Section 3): vote your sincere position, and do not sit Neutral when you actually hold a view. Abstain when a topic is outside your domain rather than vote with the room on a paper you have not read. "Heard a summary" is not "familiar with the issue."
- P2000R5 (Direction for ISO C++) cautions against voting on proposals you have not reviewed, voting from friendship or rivalry, and opposing a proposal because it competes with your own work. No one checks familiarity; this runs on the honor system.

## Reading the result: consensus, the 2:1 guideline, sustained opposition

- The real bar is the ISO consensus definition, quoted in SD-4: "general agreement, characterized by the absence of sustained opposition to substantial issues by any important part of the concerned interests." The phrase a newcomer must internalize is absence of sustained opposition. Consensus is not unanimity, and it is not a simple majority.
- Rough arithmetic guideline: a proposal normally advances with more than twice as many in favor as against. This is the 2:1 guideline in SD-4. The word "normally" plus chair discretion makes it a guideline, not a rule.
- For forwarding to the next stage, a stronger ratio is expected. P1000R6 states that strong consensus means roughly 3:1 favor to against.
- Neutral votes are excluded from the favor-to-against count.
- Edge case to explain: strong opposition carries heavy weight. Because the bar is absence of sustained opposition, a bloc of SA votes can prevent consensus even when many more people favor than oppose. Documented example: a January 2022 poll to send P2300R4 to LWG for C++23 had 37 in favor and 17 against, yet the outcome was recorded as no consensus, citing "sustained strong opposition" (P2459R0).

## Straw poll versus formal vote

- Straw polls (subgroups) gauge the room and steer the work. Formal votes (plenary motions and national-body ballots) carry procedural weight.
- Since 1991, straw votes follow the same rules as formal votes, so the committee treats them as serious predictors (N0013).
- A subgroup straw poll is a recommendation. Working-group decisions are recommendations to the parent committee (WG21 Bible, Part 1). Only a plenary motion puts wording into the working draft, and only national-body ballots turn a draft into a standard.

## Voting mechanics: show of hands, subgroups, plenary

- The committee votes by show of hands, and the tallies are recorded (d4129).
- Subgroups: everyone in the room may vote, on the five-point scale.
- Plenary: only people listed in the ISO Global Directory may vote, one person one vote. Plenary uses a simpler In Favour / Opposed / Abstain scale (N4985, d4129).
- Practical consequence: a guest or first-time attendee can vote in a subgroup straw poll but not at plenary.
- Precision note for the writer: at plenary the voters are individuals in the Global Directory, not national bodies. National bodies vote at the ballot stages (below). Avoid writing "national bodies vote at plenary"; that conflates the two layers.

## Direction versus design: two gates, and "encouragement is not approval"

- Separate direction polls from decision polls. Direction polls are revisitable guidance ("is this a good basis or good direction?"). Decision polls are binding advancement ("send this onward" or "ship it"). Take direction polls liberally; be cautious with decision polls (Reform Codex, Section 3).
- Direction approval is not shipping approval. Documented example: in October 2021 the committee reached consensus that the sender/receiver model (P2300) was a good basis for async work, then in January 2022 failed to reach consensus to ship it for C++23 (P2453R0, then P2459R0).
- "We want to see more work," or a positive direction poll, is encouragement, not a commitment that the feature will ship. A directional yes can sit for years with nothing delivered.

## The closing plenary and how a feature enters C++

- The meeting ends with a plenary where subgroups report and motions for adoption are taken.
- Plenary defaults to unanimous consent. The chair asks whether there is any objection, and silence adopts the motion. If anyone objects, the chair falls back to a counted poll. Raising a hand to object requires no explanation (Reform Codex, Section 3).
- A feature formally enters the C++ working draft when a plenary motion to adopt its wording carries.
- Subgroup approval is not the last word. A proposal can pass every subgroup straw poll and still fail at plenary. `std::byte` passed all subgroup straw polls, then failed the plenary motion on a naming objection raised by the Canadian national body (P0583R0).

## Issue processing in CWG and LWG

- CWG (Core Working Group) settles language wording. LWG (Library Working Group) settles library wording. These are the wording groups that produce specification-quality text before plenary (WG21 Bible, Part 1).

## National-body comments and ballot resolution (CD / DIS / FDIS)

- A document becomes a standard only through national-body ballots at the subcommittee (SC22) level. The stages are Committee Draft (CD), Draft International Standard (DIS), and Final Draft International Standard (FDIS). One country, one vote (WG21 Bible, Part 1).
- During balloting, national bodies file comments. A negative vote accompanied by technical comments is valid, and every comment must be addressed. This is the channel by which a national body can override or reopen a subgroup decision: the `std::byte` naming change originated from a national body, not a subgroup (P0583R0).
- Thresholds present in the sources:
  - Approval requires a two-thirds majority of P-members in favor and no more than one-quarter negative. Roughly seven negative votes among about twenty-five voting nations fails it (WG21 Bible, Part 1).
  - FDIS: if more than 25% of national-body votes are "No," the FDIS fails (N1134, d4129).

## A minority can block, and the escalation path

- Because consensus means absence of sustained opposition, a small, determined group can prevent advancement. This is structural, not a loophole. At FDIS, a quarter of national bodies voting No defeats a standard that individual participants overwhelmingly support (d4129).
- Practical reading for the newcomer: find out early who has concerns and engage them before the poll. A lone objector is weaker than a seconded one; one person generally cannot sustain a block alone without an ally who objects on the merits (Reform Codex, Section 3).
- The escalation path when you disagree with a subgroup outcome:
  1. Raise the concern early, inside the subgroup, to the chair.
  2. Put it in writing, post it to the relevant reflector, and carry it through your national body.
  3. Bring a paper. A paperless alternative carries no weight in practice (WG21 Bible, Part 1).
  4. Meet the deadline. Serious concerns should be entered by 5pm the evening before the closing plenary, or they may not be given weight (WG21 Bible, Part 1; Reform Codex, Section 3).
  5. The chain runs upward from the subgroup chair to the convener, and ultimately to the national-body ballots and the ISO appeal process. SD-4 documents WG21's own practices; the ISO/IEC Directives govern the ballot and appeal layer.
- Decisions should rest on rationale already in the public record. A new argument first raised at plenary, never debated in writing, is weak regardless of who raises it. Pre-announcing a plenary objection on the reflector builds the record and reduces surprise (Reform Codex, Section 3).

## Evening and ad-hoc sessions

- Side and ad-hoc meetings can shape direction. A two-day ad-hoc executors meeting in 2018, with about 27 self-selected attendees, set the direction that became P2300 (d4129, P1194R0).

## Facts I could not verify from the sources

- Closing plenary on "Saturday." The sources reference "Friday plenary polls" historically (1990-2014) and "the evening before closing plenary," but do not confirm the day is Saturday. Verify the meeting day before stating it.
- CWG/LWG "processing issues line by line." The CWG/LWG wording roles are confirmed, but the line-by-line issue-list mechanic is not described in these sources. Source it separately (the CWG/LWG issues lists on open-std.org).
- DIS "3-month enquiry" and FDIS "8-week approval, editorial only" durations. The two-thirds and 25%-negative thresholds are in the sources; the specific durations and the editorial-only FDIS limit are not. Confirm against the ISO/IEC Directives.
- "Evening sessions can make decisions, some groups do and some explicitly don't." Not stated in these sources. Confirm which groups permit binding decisions in evening sessions (SD-4 or subgroup practice).
- The phrase "I can live with it." Not present verbatim in these sources. The underlying idea (consensus is not unanimity, and mild dissent does not block) is fully supported by the ISO consensus definition, so the concept is safe even though the catchphrase is unverified.
