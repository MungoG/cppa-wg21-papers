# Chapter 7 fact brief: In the Room

Scope for the writer: how a newcomer should behave, speak, and find their footing during a WG21 meeting week. Keep it neutral, practical, and welcoming. The reader should think "good manners for responsible participation," never "recruited into a faction."

Sources used:
- Meeting-conduct material (WG21 "Bible" Part 8), priority source.
- Informal-dynamics notes, used only for neutral facts about hallways and the social side.
- Delegate-behavior principles (Reform Codex, Section 4 only), reframed as advice to the reader about their own conduct.

Several chapter topics are not covered by these three sources. Those are listed under "Facts I could not verify" at the end. Do not state them as fact without a separate source.

## The meeting week, in shape

- A meeting is one week of parallel subgroup sessions that converge on a closing plenary. Six or seven tracks run at the same time, and you can be in only one room at a time.
- Recent meetings draw about 200 attendees from roughly 28 to 30 nations, with about two dozen brand-new guests each time. The format is hybrid: people attend both onsite and online. (Counts come from the Brno June 2026 and Sofia June 2025 trip reports.)
- New attendees should go to the Sunday 6pm orientation. It sets the week's logistics and norms. (do)
- Before you walk into any room, read that room's agenda papers. Arriving without having read them is the classic rookie mistake. (don't)
- You cannot follow six tracks at once. Pick one and prepare for it. If your national body sends more than one person, split the rooms and trade notes at day's end.

## The opening and closing plenary

- The week brackets the subgroup work with plenary sessions. Adopted wording goes into the working draft at the closing plenary.
- Only names in the ISO LiveLink global directory cast plenary votes. Guests take full part in subgroup work but do not vote in plenary.
- FLAG: the specific opening-plenary sequence in the chapter outline (officers introduced, a national-body poll, agenda approval, then disperse to subgroups) is not described in the sources. Confirm it from SD-4 or a recent trip report before stating it. See flags.

## Moving between rooms

- You attend one room at a time while six or seven run in parallel, so your day is a sequence of choices about where to be.
- Follow the agenda. You go where the paper you care about is scheduled, so you may move between rooms across the week rather than camping in one.
- FLAG: "spend time in rooms outside your specialty," attributed to the Direction paper P0939, is not in the sources. The meeting-mechanics source actually stresses focusing on the one room you prepared for. List P0939 as a resource, but pull that specific advice from P0939 itself, not from here.

## Speaking in the room

- Sessions are working discussions: presentation, debate, straw polls, and revisions through the week.
- FLAG: the chapter's speaking etiquette is not stated in the sources. That includes raising your hand to be recognized, keeping a point to about two minutes, stating your name when you start so scribes and remote attendees can follow, and using the microphone because remote participants cannot hear you without it.
- Two enabling facts are sourced and support those norms: meetings are hybrid with remote participants, and every session is minuted by a scribe. Use them to motivate the norms, but source the actual rules (likely the meeting guidelines N4916 or the Code of Conduct) before presenting them as rules.

## Confidentiality: recording, posting, photos

- Meeting records, committee wikis, and reflector (mailing-list) posts are protected materials under SD-4. You may not quote them publicly, in papers or blog posts, except through two narrow gates (see the next cluster).
- The meeting guidelines (N4916) bar live tweeting, blogging, photographs, and video from sessions. Recording is not permitted. (don't)
- Practical posture for a newcomer: assume what is said in the room stays off the public record unless a specific exception applies.

## What you can and cannot quote

- Poll questions and their numeric results: quote freely, no permission needed. (do)
- A specific person's words or stated position: quote only with that person's prior consent. (do, with consent)
- Everything else: paraphrase only. (default)
- For the writer: in practice, asking for and granting quoting consent is rare, so paraphrase is the working default. State this plainly and briefly. Do not import any editorial framing about why the consent gate goes unused. Keep it factual.

## Note-taking and minutes

- Every session must be minuted, telecons included. Minutes are not optional for the group.
- Any participant may be asked to take minutes, so a newcomer can be handed the task. Useful short quote: "any participant may be asked to take minutes."
- Volunteering to scribe is a low-stakes, welcome way to contribute and to learn how a room runs. (do)

## The social side: hallways and meals

- The hallway is not only social time. A lot of the real work and relationship-building happens there, between sessions.
- Talking with people informally, before you ever present a paper cold, is normal and expected, not pushy. Introducing yourself and asking questions is how a newcomer gets known.
- FLAG (partial): the chapter specifics are not detailed in the sources. That includes evening sessions, dinners, and lunch or dinner groups that form organically and that you should join. The general point holds and is supported: relationships form in the informal spaces around the sessions. The specific meal and evening logistics need another source or the writer's own experience.

## Four habits of conduct (delegate-behavior principles)

Reframed as advice to the reader about their own conduct. Neutral, no faction. Drawn only from the delegate-behavior section.

- Service before doctrine. Contribute by serving the community. Help onboard newcomers, write paper tutorials, explain procedure. How you show up sets the tone for who joins. Short label worth using: "Service before doctrine."
- Evidence discipline from day one. Bring implementation evidence, deployment data, or field experience to every paper, even your first. Arrive with running code rather than "implementation experience: none."
- Name your baseline. Before your second meeting, write down what you currently consider normal about how the committee works. Revisit it after a year or two. The change tells you how your own judgment has shifted.
- Ask how other bodies handle it. Do not accept "this is just how it works" as a final answer. Ask how IETF, W3C, or TC39 handle the same thing. The comparison tells you whether a practice is principled or simply inherited.
- Optional self-check: notice whether your first instinct on a paper is "where is this in the pipeline?" or "is this any good?" Reaching for process first is worth noticing in yourself.
