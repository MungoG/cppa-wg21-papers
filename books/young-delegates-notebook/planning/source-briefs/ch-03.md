# Chapter 3 Fact Brief: How Papers Work

Condensed source material for the writer. Facts only, no book prose. Drawn from the WG21 Bible (Parts 3, 4, 8) and the formal-process notes in `wg21-milieu.md`. Items the four sources could not confirm are collected under "Flagged" at the end.

## The Paper Is the Unit of Work

- SD-4 rule, stated three times across the sources: if a proposal does not have a paper, it does not exist. Routing, discussion, and voting all happen to a paper, never to an idea in a hallway.
- A proposal is only actionable when three things hold at once: a paper exists, it arrived before the mailing deadline, and a qualified presenter is in the room. Miss any one and the room cannot act on it.
- A competing design with no paper does not block a design that has one. A concern raised without a paper carries little weight.
- The paper is the instrument that makes the decision, not a write-up of a decision already made. It must work for two readers at once: the busy delegate with five minutes and the one with thirty.

## Types of Papers

The sources sort papers along several axes.

- By intent (a front-matter field). Two kinds:
  - Ask-paper: proposes, requests a poll, seeks adoption. Declares `intent: ask`.
  - Inform-paper: places evidence in the record and stops. Declares `intent: info`. This is where data papers and experience reports live. A paper that asks for nothing tends to get read, because it adds no burden to the chair's calendar instead of competing for scarce floor time.
- By mode: design papers vs wording papers (see routing below).
- By document prefix: P, N, or D (see the P-number system below).
- Standing documents (SD-n): governance and reference documents, not proposals. The four that matter here:
  - SD-4, WG21 Practices and Procedures: the rulebook.
  - SD-7, Mailing Procedures and How to Write Papers: how to get a number, submit, and format. Governs form, not evidence.
  - SD-9, Library Evolution Policies: recurring library defaults (for example, when a function should be `[[nodiscard]]` or `noexcept`, naming conventions). Set by LEWG.
  - SD-10, Language Evolution Principles: the design values EWG argues from (zero-overhead, no viral annotation, no library bifurcation, safe by default with opt-out, and others).
- Direction papers: P0939, the Direction Group's priorities document. [Flagged: the four sources do not describe P0939 or the Direction Group.]

## The P-Number System: P vs N vs D, and Revisions

- P number: a proposal (for example, P1234).
- N number: an administrative document such as an agenda or minutes.
- D prefix: a pre-publication draft that does not resolve publicly. Cite the published P revision, never the D draft.
- Revision suffix: the first published version is R0, then R1, R2, and up. Always read the highest revision available.
- What a revision number tells you: how many review cycles the paper has been through. R0 is brand new. A high number means the paper has been revised repeatedly in response to feedback. It signals iteration history, not correctness.
- Getting and formatting a number is governed by SD-7.
- wg21.link resolves every published number. URL pattern, exactly as the source writes it: `https://wg21.link/p1234r0`. Lowercase, with the revision. It is run by a committee delegate and has shortened paper links for years.

## Where a Paper Goes: the Routing Grid

- Every proposal lands in a two-by-two grid. Axes: Domain (library or language) and Mode (design or wording).
  - Library + design routes to LEWG (Library Evolution Working Group).
  - Library + wording routes to LWG (Library Working Group).
  - Language + design routes to EWG (Evolution Working Group).
  - Language + wording routes to CWG (Core Working Group).
- The grid is multi-label. A paper can land in more than one cell. A new keyword that also needs library support routes to EWG and LEWG. A design paper that carries its own wording visits LEWG, then LWG.
- The cell sets the bar. A library cell answers to SD-9 policy defaults. A language cell answers to SD-10 principles.
- Routing reads sustained signal, not a stray mention. One sentence about overload resolution does not turn a library paper into a language paper.

## The Paper Lifecycle: Idea to International Standard

Formal pipeline, in order:

1. The author submits a paper to an open-std.org mailing.
2. Study groups (SG1 through SG23) incubate the idea.
3. Design groups give directional approval: EWG for core language, LEWG for library.
4. Wording groups turn the approved design into specification-quality text: CWG for core, LWG for library.
5. Plenary: the full committee votes to adopt the wording into the working draft.
6. Ballots. The working draft becomes a DIS (Draft International Standard), a three-month enquiry ballot needing a two-thirds majority of P-member national bodies. Comments are resolved. Then an FDIS (Final Draft International Standard), an eight-week approval vote on editorial comments only.
7. Publication as an ISO International Standard.

- The train model: since C++11, a new standard ships every three years. C++11, 14, 17, 20, 23, 26, 29. P1000 (Sutter) codifies the cadence.
- Evidentiary floor: SD-4 states there is no ISO requirement that something be implemented before it is standardized. A feature can in principle move from a design sketch to the standard with no users outside the proposing organization.

## Design Review vs Wording Review

Two stages, two different skills and mindsets.

- Design review (EWG / LEWG): is this the right feature? The question is direction and merit, measured against SD-10 principles for language or SD-9 policies for library.
- Wording review (CWG / LWG): is the specification text precise, unambiguous, and complete on edge cases? The question is spec quality, not whether the feature is wanted.
- The design-to-wording handoff: once the design group approves the direction, the wording group takes over and produces the standard text. A design paper that carries its own wording visits the design group first, then the wording group.
- Review questions are gated by paper type. A pure wording paper does not face the motivation or rationale lens. A defect report does not face the implementation-evidence lens.

## The Mailing System

- Volume: the committee publishes 300 to 500 papers a year across pre-meeting and post-meeting mailings.
- Two places to find it:
  - open-std.org: the official archive. A flat chronological list of PDFs, with no filter, no search, and no grouping.
  - wg21.org: an enhanced view of the same papers, filtered by subgroup, full-text searchable, grouped into per-room reports, and linked back to the open-std.org originals. Volunteer-built, not an official committee site. The papers still live on open-std.org.
- Pre-meeting mailing deadline: papers must arrive before it. A late paper is not actionable and will not be discussed during meeting time.

## Reading a Paper Critically

- Read in proportion to where you vote. Nobody reads all 300 to 500. Filter to your subgroup and leave the rest until it reaches a poll you cast.
- Read the latest revision. A stale revision leads you to raise a concern the author already resolved.
- When a paper returns, compare against the prior revision so you read what changed, not the whole paper again.
- The narrow-lens questions to run on any paper:
  - Which group should review this?
  - Is the motivation stated, and are alternatives acknowledged?
  - Does it cite and position against prior art?
  - Can a competent C++ programmer understand it from the paper alone?
  - Does it affect ABI, and is the impact discussed?
  - Does it have safety implications, and are they addressed?
  - What existing code breaks, and is migration addressed?
  - Are the claims backed by implementation experience?
  - Is the proposed wording unambiguous, with edge cases covered?
- Red flags are empty answers to those questions: no prior-art positioning, no implementation evidence, no migration story. Each gap is an objection someone will raise on the floor.
- Common mistakes: trying to read everything and reading nothing well; reading the abstract and voting on the title; reading a stale revision.

## Defect Reports and Issues Lists

- A defect report fixes a bug in the already-published standard. Because it corrects wording rather than proposing a feature, it is not held to the implementation-evidence lens in review.

## Documents Every Delegate Should Read

- SD-4 (WG21 Practices and Procedures): the rulebook everyone is expected to know. It carries the "no paper, no proposal" rule, the mailing-deadline and presenter requirements, the five-point straw-poll scale (Strongly Favor, Weakly Favor, Neutral, Weakly Against, Strongly Against), the guideline that a proposal normally advances when those in favor outnumber those against by more than two to one, the consensus definition ("absence of sustained opposition"), the escalation path for objections, the protected-materials rules, and the statement that implementation is not required before standardization.
- P0939 (Direction for ISO C++), the Direction Group's priorities document. [Flagged: not covered by the four sources.]
